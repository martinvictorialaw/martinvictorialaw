Winning the Name, Keeping the Control

What the Charter-Cox Stockholders Agreement Reveals About Leverage

Usually, when one company buys another, the buyer's name survives. In the Charter-Cox deal, it is the other way around: by August 2027, Charter must rename itself Cox Communications. The Stockholders Agreement shows that Cox traded control of its business for continuing influence and protected value, and the drafting reveals who held leverage on each term. What Cox wanted most, its name and its Atlanta presence, is locked in with mandatory language, while what Charter kept, the Spectrum brand and its Stamford headquarters, stays in Charter's hands through discretionary language. However, most of Cox's protections are temporary, and they depend on Cox remaining a major holder, Charter remaining independent, or both.

On August 19, 2026, Charter Communications completed its transaction with Cox Enterprises, under which Charter acquired Cox's businesses. In exchange, a Cox subsidiary received approximately 33.6 million common units in Charter Communications Holdings, LLC, a Charter subsidiary; $6 billion of convertible preferred units in the same subsidiary with a 6.875 percent coupon; and approximately $4.2 billion in cash. At the $353.64 reference price used in the transaction, the common units were worth about $11.9 billion, bringing Cox's total consideration to roughly $22 billion. The cash was therefore only a small portion of the total, likely because about $12 billion of Cox's debt and finance leases also remained outstanding at Charter's subsidiaries. Cash is more appealing to sellers because it is certain, but more of it likely would have required Charter to borrow even more on top of the debt it was already taking on. Even if this structure was a necessity for Charter rather than a choice, Cox still had to agree to take less than a fifth of its consideration in cash. At the units' lower implied value at closing, the cash share rises to about 28 percent, but the parties set the terms using the reference price.

The key terms appear in Sections 2.10 and 2.11 of the Third Amended and Restated Stockholders Agreement (the "Stockholders Agreement"), which Charter, Cox, a Cox subsidiary, and Advance/Newhouse Partnership (A/N), Charter's other major shareholder, signed at closing. Under Section 2.10, Charter must take all actions necessary to change its name to Cox Communications, Inc. no later than one year after closing, meaning by August 19, 2027. Section 2.10 also requires the name to remain for at least two years after closing, and after that, any change needs the approval of at least two-thirds of the full board, subject to narrow exceptions such as material harm to the reputation of the Cox name. However, this two-year requirement applies only in the absence of a Company Change of Control, meaning a buyer of Charter would not be bound by it. Similarly, under Section 2.11, Charter must establish a significant corporate presence at the Cox campus in Atlanta, with 2,000 employees or fewer if fewer were there at closing, within one year of closing and keep it for at least two years after closing. That commitment is subject to the same change-of-control exception, and it ends if Cox's equity and voting interests both fall below 20 percent.

By contrast, under Sections 2.10 and 2.11, Charter is to continue operating nationally under the Spectrum brand and to remain headquartered in Stamford, Connecticut, "unless otherwise determined by the Company." As a result, the Cox name moves onto the public company, while customers in every market, including Cox's former markets, will see the Spectrum brand. Charter also kept its leadership, with Chris Winfrey continuing as President and CEO.

Further proof of Charter's push to keep control through the transaction is found in the voting provisions of Sections 2.2 and 2.3 of the Stockholders Agreement. Under Section 2.2(a), Cox has the right to designate three of Charter's thirteen board members as long as its equity or voting interest is at least 20 percent. This may seem to give Cox real power over Charter's decisions, but it is less than it appears. Under Section 2.3(a)(i), most board actions require only a majority of the full board, so Cox's three seats are unlikely to change the outcome of a vote on their own. The decisions listed in Section 2.3(a)(ii), such as a transaction between Charter and Cox or an amendment to Charter's certificate of incorporation, also require approval from a majority of the unaffiliated directors, who must be independent of both Cox and A/N. Cox did secure some governance rights of its own, but as discussed below, those rights are also limited.

The protections Cox won were built to fade. Because of the change-of-control carve-outs in Sections 2.10 and 2.11, a buyer of Charter would not be bound by the two-year name requirement or the Atlanta commitment. That suggests the parties expected this arrangement to matter only while Charter remained independent, and that Cox accepted the risk of a sale it could not fully block. Cox's governance rights follow the same pattern. Under Section 2.9, Alex Taylor, Cox's designee, chairs the board for no more than three years, after which the role is set to pass to Winfrey. Under Section 2.5, Cox keeps its seat on the committee that would select Charter's next CEO only while its equity or voting interest is at least 11 percent.

The 20 percent threshold works the same way. Cox now holds about 26 percent of Charter, leaving it roughly six points of room. Charter cannot dilute Cox below that line through a cash offering without giving Cox the chance to buy in. Section 4.1(a) of the Stockholders Agreement gives Cox preemptive rights to buy its proportionate share of any new equity Charter sells for cash, and that right lasts as long as Cox's equity interest is at least 10 percent. As a result, the most likely way Cox falls below 20 percent is by choosing to sell part of its stake. The Atlanta commitment ends only if both Cox's equity and voting interests drop below the threshold, so unless Charter issues new stock for something other than cash, such as an acquisition, the protection is largely in Cox's own hands.

Even while Cox's protections are in force, some of the language behind them leaves room for disagreement. Section 2.11 requires Charter to have "a significant corporate presence" at the Cox campus in Atlanta with 2,000 employees, but the agreement never defines the phrase. It is unclear whether 2,000 employees is enough on its own, or whether a corporate presence also requires leadership and decision-making roles. For example, if Charter kept 2,000 customer service employees in Atlanta but moved every executive role to Stamford, Charter could argue that it met the headcount, while Cox could argue that Charter no longer has a corporate presence there. The wording of Section 2.11 adds to the problem. The first sentence requires a "significant corporate presence" at the Cox campus, but the sentence that makes the commitment last two years refers only to a "significant presence in Atlanta," dropping both "corporate" and the reference to the Cox campus. Because Section 7.6 of the Stockholders Agreement provides that Delaware law governs, a court would generally give undefined terms their plain meaning, often by looking to dictionaries. However, if the language can reasonably be read more than one way, a court may look at outside evidence such as the parties' negotiations. Since "corporate" could refer either to the company as a whole or to its management, a court could find the phrase ambiguous.

Cox's consent right under Section 2.7(b)(i)(B) of the Stockholders Agreement raises a similar problem. While Cox's equity or voting interest is at least 20 percent, Charter cannot, without Cox's prior written consent, "fundamentally change the business or material investments of the Company to an extent that would constitute a significant departure from the Company's existing business." Neither "fundamentally change" nor "significant departure" is defined. It is unclear whether a major expansion of Spectrum Mobile would count, or whether selling the commercial fiber business Charter just bought from Cox would. The clause directly above it shows that the drafters could be precise when they wanted to be. Under that clause, Charter needs Cox's consent to take on new debt, other than refinancing, that would push its leverage ratio above 4.5 times, or 4.0 times after three years. This suggests the parties agreed that Cox should have a say in any major change in direction but could not agree on what that change would look like. As a result, the consent right is one of Cox's most important protections, but also one of its least clear.

The obvious response is that the name change is mostly symbolic. Customers will still see Spectrum, the company will still be run from Stamford, and Cox's board seats are unlikely to decide a vote. However, that view overlooks what Cox actually received. Cox's name will sit on one of the largest cable companies in the country, its designee chairs the board for up to three years, and while Cox holds at least 20 percent, its consent is needed before Charter exceeds set debt limits or changes direction. Cox also took on real risk to get there. Its common units were valued at about $11.9 billion at the transaction's reference price, but Charter put their implied value at roughly $5 billion at closing. By taking most of its consideration in equity, Cox tied its return to Charter's performance, which helps explain why it would want influence over the company rather than simply more cash.

For deal lawyers, the agreement offers three lessons. First, the choice between mandatory and discretionary language often shows who had leverage on each term, and reading the two side by side can reveal more than either one alone. Second, a seller that accepts equity should look closely at what its protections are tied to. Cox's rights depend on its ownership staying above set thresholds and on Charter remaining independent. A seller in Cox's position could ask that its key commitments bind any future buyer of the company, or that a sale be conditioned on the buyer assuming them. Third, vague terms like "significant corporate presence" invite disputes. Cox could have tried to close that gap by defining the presence to include specific roles, such as a minimum number of senior executives or the headquarters of a named business unit, rather than relying on headcount alone. Where possible, drafters should use measurable standards, as the parties did with the leverage ratio.

The first real test comes on August 19, 2027, when Charter must have taken the Cox name. Whether that happens smoothly, and whether the Atlanta presence still looks like a corporate one when the two-year minimum runs out in August 2028, will show how much the mandatory language was worth. Until then, the agreement is a reminder that in a deal like this one, the seller can win the name and still leave the buyer in control.
